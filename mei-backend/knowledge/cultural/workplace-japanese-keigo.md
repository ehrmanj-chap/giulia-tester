# Workplace Japanese and Keigo

Source seed: Kelsie Kato's Mei Cultural Intelligence research folder, file “Workplace Japanese and Keigo - Additional Research.pdf”, updated September 7, 2026.

Japan's Agency for Cultural Affairs' official Guidelines on Keigo distinguishes five categories rather than the simplified three-part framework often taught to learners:

- 尊敬語 (sonkeigo): respectful language that elevates the person being discussed and their actions.
- 謙譲語I (kenjōgo I): humble language for the speaker's own action when that action is directed toward someone being shown respect.
- 謙譲語II / 丁重語 (kenjōgo II / teichōgo): deferential description of the speaker's own action to the listener more generally.
- 丁寧語 (teineigo): general polite language, including familiar -desu/-masu forms.
- 美化語 (bikago): beautified/polite noun forms, often using お / ご prefixes.

Examples documented in the source include いく → いらっしゃる for sonkeigo and いく → まいる for kenjōgo II/teichōgo.

The Agency for Cultural Affairs frames contemporary keigo as relationship- and context-sensitive self-expression, not simply a fixed ranking system. Its guidance explicitly situates honorific language within a society based on mutually recognized equal personhood. For practical advice, avoid reducing keigo to “lower person speaks humbly to higher person.” The relationship, setting, and communicative intent matter.

The Agency's interactive keigo materials also emphasize common overcorrection errors: learners can misuse humble forms for someone else's action or stack honorific constructions unnecessarily. This supports teaching keigo through contextual examples rather than memorizing isolated transformations.

The research also notes Tokyo Metropolitan Government programs offering Business Japanese and Business Manners instruction to eligible foreign residents, and MHLW resources including multilingual workplace/labor terminology and consultation channels. Eligibility and program details are time-sensitive and should not be presented as current without checking a dated source.

JETRO guidance cited in the research connects polite/indirect language with negotiation: highly polite wording can still communicate a substantive refusal or firm constraint. Politeness should not be interpreted automatically as flexibility or agreement.
